Reactions

The Class 11–12 organic reactions, each with the role every participating element plays in it. The same element changes role from reaction to reaction: bromine is an electrophile in Br₂ addition, a nucleophile as Br⁻, a radical with peroxide, and a leaving group in substitution.

Role index

The same element plays different roles in different reactions. Codes point to the reactions below.

| Element | Roles it plays, with reaction codes |
| H | Electrophile H⁺ (H2, O2, O5); radical H• abstracted (H1) or delivered (H3); acidic proton removed (H7, O1, P1, C5, A2); hydride H⁻ donor (C3, C7); proton lost to restore aromaticity (H9) |
| C | Nucleophile through π electrons (H2, H4, H9); carbocation (H2, X2, O2); free radical (H1, H3); carbanion (H7, X6, C5); electrophilic centre δ+ (X1, C1, O6, O7); migrating R group (A1) |
| O | Nucleophile (H5, X1, O6, O7); base that accepts H⁺ (O2, O8); leaving group as H₂O after protonation (O2, O6); +R ring activator (P2, P3, P4, P5); takes negative charge in C=O addition (C1) |
| N | Nucleophile (A2, A3, A4, A5, A8); base (A8: binds AlCl₃); +R ring activator (A7, A8); N₂ as leaving group (A6); attacking atom of an ambident nucleophile (X5) |

Hydrocarbons

H1. Free-radical halogenation of alkanes

Equation: CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl (hν or heat); further substitution gives CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃, CCl₄.

Type: free-radical substitution, chain mechanism.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| Cl (in Cl₂) | Radical (Cl•) | Light breaks the weak Cl–Cl bond homolytically |
| H (in C–H) | Abstracted as H• by Cl• | Forms the strong H–Cl bond |
| C | Becomes a free radical (CH₃•) | Left with 7 electrons after losing H• |

1. Initiation: Cl₂ → 2Cl•.
2. Propagation: Cl• + CH₄ → CH₃• + HCl; CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•.
3. Termination: Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂; CH₃• + CH₃• → CH₃–CH₃; CH₃• + Cl• → CH₃Cl.

- Ethane traces in the product come from the CH₃• + CH₃• termination step.
- Reactivity of halogens: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. Iodination is reversible and needs an oxidising agent.
- Ease of H abstraction: 3° > 2° > 1° (follows radical stability); bromination is far more selective than chlorination.

H2. Addition of HBr to an unsymmetrical alkene (Markovnikov)

Equation: CH₃–CH=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃–CHBr–CH₃ (2-bromopropane, major).

Type: electrophilic addition, ionic.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| H (from HBr) | Electrophile (H⁺), attacks first | Polar H–Br bond; H⁺ seeks the π electrons |
| C=C | Nucleophile (π electrons), then carbocation | Loosely held π electrons donate to H⁺ |
| Br | Nucleophile (Br⁻), attacks second | Has lone pairs and a negative charge |

1. H⁺ adds to the CH₂ end, giving the 2° carbocation CH₃–C⁺H–CH₃ (more stable than the 1° alternative).
2. Br⁻ attacks the carbocation → 2-bromopropane.

- Markovnikov's rule: the negative part of the reagent goes to the carbon with fewer hydrogens.
- Rate of HX addition: HI > HBr > HCl.

H3. Peroxide effect (anti-Markovnikov, Kharasch)

Equation: CH₃–CH=CH₂ + HBr (peroxide) → CH₃–CH₂–CH₂Br (1-bromopropane).

Type: free-radical addition.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| Peroxide (R–O–O–R) | Initiator | Weak O–O bond breaks homolytically into RO• |
| Br | Radical (Br•), attacks first | RO• takes H• from HBr, releasing Br• |
| C=C | Becomes a 2° radical | Br• adds to the CH₂ end, giving the more stable radical |
| H (from HBr) | Delivered last, as H• | The 2° radical takes H• from another HBr, regenerating Br• |

- Works only with HBr. With HCl the H–Cl bond is too strong; with HI, I• combines to I₂ rather than adding.

H4. Addition of bromine (test for unsaturation)

Equation: CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ (in CCl₄) → BrCH₂–CH₂Br (1,2-dibromoethane). Red-brown colour disappears.

Type: electrophilic addition.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| Br₂ | Electrophile (one Br becomes δ+) | The approaching π cloud polarises Br–Br |
| C=C | Nucleophile | π electrons attack the δ+ Br |
| Br⁻ (second Br) | Nucleophile | Completes the addition on the other carbon |

- Alkynes add two molecules of Br₂ (tetrabromide).

H5. Hydration of alkynes

Equation: HC≡CH + H₂O → CH₃CHO (dil. H₂SO₄, HgSO₄, 333 K).

Type: electrophilic addition (Markovnikov), then tautomerism.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| Hg²⁺ | Catalyst | Activates the triple bond towards water |
| O (of H₂O) | Nucleophile | Lone pair adds to the carbon |
| H | Moves from O to C in tautomerism | Enol (C=C–OH) is unstable and becomes the keto form |

- Ethyne gives acetaldehyde; propyne and higher alkynes give ketones (propyne → acetone).

H6. Hydrogenation and partial reduction

| Reaction | Reagent / conditions | Product | Role of the metal |
| Alkene or alkyne + H₂ | Pt or Pd at room temperature; Ni at 523–573 K | Alkane | Catalyst surface adsorbs H₂ and the hydrocarbon |
| Alkyne + H₂ | Lindlar's catalyst (Pd/C poisoned with S compounds or quinoline) | cis-Alkene | Poisoned catalyst stops after one addition |
| Alkyne | Na in liquid NH₃ | trans-Alkene | Na donates electrons (dissolving-metal reduction) |

H7. Acidity of terminal alkynes

Equation: HC≡CH + NaNH₂ → HC≡C⁻Na⁺ + NH₃ (or Na metal: + ½H₂).

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| H (on sp carbon) | Acidic proton, removed | sp carbon (50% s-character) holds the electron pair of the C–H bond tightly |
| C (sp) | Becomes a carbanion (acetylide) | Keeps the bonding pair |
| NH₂⁻ | Base | Stronger base than acetylide |

- Acidity: HC≡CH > CH₂=CH₂ > CH₃–CH₃. Alkenes and alkanes do not react with Na or NaNH₂.

H8. Ozonolysis

Equation: alkene + O₃ → ozonide; ozonide + Zn/H₂O → two carbonyl compounds.

- Each carbon of the C=C becomes a C=O. Zn destroys the H₂O₂ formed, so the aldehydes are not oxidised further to acids.
- Used to find the position of a double bond: CH₃CH=CH₂ → CH₃CHO + HCHO.

H9. Electrophilic substitution of benzene

General path: the electrophile (E⁺) attacks the π cloud → arenium ion (σ-complex, one sp³ carbon, resonance-stabilised) → loss of H⁺ restores aromaticity.

| Reaction | Reagents | Electrophile and how it forms | Product |
| Nitration | conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄ | NO₂⁺: HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻ | Nitrobenzene |
| Halogenation | Cl₂, anhydrous FeCl₃ or AlCl₃ | Cl⁺: Cl₂ + AlCl₃ → Cl⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ | Chlorobenzene |
| Sulphonation | fuming H₂SO₄ | SO₃ | Benzenesulphonic acid (reversible) |
| Friedel–Crafts alkylation | RCl, anhydrous AlCl₃ | R⁺ | Alkylbenzene |
| Friedel–Crafts acylation | RCOCl, anhydrous AlCl₃ | RCO⁺ (acylium ion) | Aryl ketone |

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| Benzene C | Nucleophile | Delocalised π electrons |
| H on the ring | Leaves as H⁺ (not as hydride) | Its loss restores the aromatic sextet |
| AlCl₃ / FeCl₃ / H₂SO₄ | Lewis or Brønsted acid; makes E⁺ | Electron-deficient Al or Fe accepts a lone pair |

- Benzene prefers substitution over addition: addition would destroy aromatic stability.
- Activating, o/p directing: –OH, –NH₂, –OR, –NHCOCH₃, –CH₃. Deactivating, meta directing: –NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COOH, –SO₃H. Halogens: deactivating but o/p directing.
- Friedel–Crafts fails on nitrobenzene, benzoic acid and aniline.

H10. Making alkanes from other compounds

| Reaction | Equation | Note |
| Wurtz | 2RX + 2Na → R–R + 2NaX (dry ether) | Na transfers electrons; methane cannot be made |
| Decarboxylation | RCOONa + NaOH → RH + Na₂CO₃ (CaO, heat) | One carbon fewer |
| Kolbe electrolysis | 2RCOONa + 2H₂O → R–R + 2CO₂ + H₂ + 2NaOH | R• radicals couple at the anode |
| Hydrogenation | Alkene/alkyne + H₂ (Pt, Pd or Ni) | See H6 |

Haloalkanes and haloarenes

X1. SN2 (bimolecular nucleophilic substitution)

Equation: CH₃Br + OH⁻ → CH₃OH + Br⁻. Rate = k[CH₃Br][OH⁻].

Type: one step; nucleophile attacks as the leaving group departs.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| O (in OH⁻) | Nucleophile, attacks from the back | Lone pairs and negative charge; the front is blocked by Br |
| C (attached to Br) | Electrophilic centre (δ+) | Br pulls electron density away |
| Br | Leaving group (Br⁻) | Stable anion; C–Br weaker than C–Cl |
| H atoms on C | Flip to the other side | Walden inversion of configuration |

- Transition state: C partly bonded to both OH and Br (five partial bonds).
- Reactivity: CH₃X > 1° > 2° > 3° (steric hindrance). Favoured by strong nucleophiles and polar aprotic solvents (acetone).
- Leaving group: I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻.

X2. SN1 (unimolecular nucleophilic substitution)

Equation: (CH₃)₃CBr + OH⁻ → (CH₃)₃COH + Br⁻. Rate = k[(CH₃)₃CBr].

Type: two steps through a carbocation.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| Br | Leaves first (slow step) | Ionisation helped by polar protic solvent |
| C | Carbocation, sp², planar | 3° carbocation stabilised by +I and hyperconjugation of three CH₃ groups |
| O (in OH⁻ or H₂O) | Nucleophile, attacks either face | Planar carbocation is open on both sides |
| Solvent (H₂O, alcohols) | Stabilises the ions | Hydrogen bonding to the leaving anion and solvation of the cation |

1. Slow: (CH₃)₃C–Br → (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Br⁻.
2. Fast: (CH₃)₃C⁺ + OH⁻ → (CH₃)₃COH.

- Reactivity: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃X; allylic and benzylic halides react fast (resonance-stabilised carbocations).
- At a chiral carbon: racemisation (both enantiomers form).
- Nucleophile concentration does not change the rate.

X3. Elimination: dehydrohalogenation

Equation: CH₃CH₂CHBrCH₃ + KOH (alc.) → CH₃CH=CHCH₃ (major) + CH₃CH₂CH=CH₂ (minor) + KBr + H₂O.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| OH⁻ / C₂H₅O⁻ (alcoholic KOH) | Base: removes a β-H | In alcohol it is poorly solvated and acts as a strong base |
| H on β-carbon | Leaves as H⁺ | Removed by the base |
| Br | Leaving group | Departs as the C=C forms |

- Saytzeff (Zaitsev) rule: the more substituted alkene is the major product.
- Same reagent, different role: aqueous KOH → OH⁻ acts as a nucleophile (substitution); alcoholic KOH → base (elimination).
- 3° halides favour elimination; 1° halides favour substitution.

X4. Halogen exchange

| Reaction | Equation | What drives it |
| Finkelstein | R–Cl/R–Br + NaI → R–I + NaCl/NaBr (dry acetone) | NaCl and NaBr are insoluble in acetone and precipitate |
| Swarts | R–Cl/R–Br + AgF, Hg₂F₂, CoF₂ or SbF₃ → R–F | Metallic fluoride supplies F |

X5. Ambident nucleophiles

| Reagent | Attacking atom | Product | Why |
| KCN | C | Alkyl cyanide R–C≡N | Mainly ionic; C attacks, and a C–C bond is more stable |
| AgCN | N | Isocyanide R–N≡C | Mainly covalent; only the N lone pair is free |
| KNO₂ | O | Alkyl nitrite R–O–N=O | Ionic; O attacks |
| AgNO₂ | N | Nitroalkane R–NO₂ | Covalent; N lone pair attacks |

X6. Grignard reagent

Formation: R–X + Mg → R–MgX (dry ether). Mg inserts between C and X.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| C bonded to Mg | Carbanion-like: nucleophile and strong base | Mg (EN 1.31) is far less electronegative than C (2.55), so C carries δ− |
| Mg | Electropositive partner; ends as Mg(OH)X | Gives up electron density |
| Water / any acidic H | Destroys the reagent: RMgX + H₂O → RH + Mg(OH)X | Carbanion takes H⁺ |

- Uses with carbonyls and CO₂: see Aldehydes, ketones and carboxylic acids (C2).

X7. Reactions of haloarenes

| Reaction | Conditions | Product | Role of the halogen |
| Dow process | Chlorobenzene + NaOH, 623 K, 300 atm, then H⁺ | Phenol | Leaves only under harsh conditions: C–Cl has partial double-bond character |
| Activated substitution | –NO₂ at o/p; aq. NaOH, milder heating | Nitrophenols | Leaves easily: –NO₂ stabilises the negative intermediate |
| Electrophilic substitution | Cl₂/FeCl₃, HNO₃/H₂SO₄, H₂SO₄, RCl/AlCl₃ | Mainly ortho and para products | Stays; deactivates the ring (−I) but directs o/p (+R) |
| Wurtz–Fittig | ArX + RX + 2Na, dry ether | Ar–R | Leaves as NaX |
| Fittig | 2ArX + 2Na, dry ether | Ar–Ar | Leaves as NaX |

X8. Chloroform and iodoform

- CHCl₃ + O₂ (light) → COCl₂ (phosgene) + HCl: store in dark, full bottles.
- CHCl₃ is the carbon source in the carbylamine test (A3) and the Reimer–Tiemann reaction (P4).
- CHI₃ (yellow) is the product of the iodoform test (C6).

Alcohols, phenols and ethers

O1. Alcohol + sodium (alcohol as an acid)

Equation: 2ROH + 2Na → 2RONa + H₂.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| H (of O–H) | Acidic proton, released as H₂ | Polar O–H bond |
| O | Becomes alkoxide (RO⁻), a strong base and nucleophile | Keeps the bonding pair |
| Na | Electron donor | Very low ionisation energy |

- Acidity of alcohols: 1° > 2° > 3° (+I of alkyl groups destabilises the alkoxide). Alcohols are weaker acids than water.

O2. Acid-catalysed dehydration

Equation: C₂H₅OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O (conc. H₂SO₄, 443 K). At 413 K: 2C₂H₅OH → C₂H₅OC₂H₅ + H₂O.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| O (of –OH) | Base: accepts H⁺, then leaves as H₂O | Protonation turns poor leaving group –OH into good leaving group –OH₂⁺ |
| C | Carbocation | Formed when water leaves |
| H on β-carbon | Lost as H⁺ | Forms the C=C |

1. ROH + H⁺ → ROH₂⁺. 2. ROH₂⁺ → R⁺ + H₂O (slow). 3. R⁺ loses β-H⁺ → alkene.

- Ease: 3° > 2° > 1°. The carbocation may rearrange before losing H⁺.
- In ether formation (413 K), a second alcohol molecule attacks as a nucleophile (SN2 on the protonated alcohol).

O3. Alcohol to alkyl halide; Lucas test

| Reagent | Equation | Note |
| HX (ZnCl₂ for HCl) | ROH + HX → RX + H₂O | Order of HX reactivity: HI > HBr > HCl |
| PCl₅ | ROH + PCl₅ → RCl + POCl₃ + HCl |  |
| PCl₃ | 3ROH + PCl₃ → 3RCl + H₃PO₃ |  |
| SOCl₂ | ROH + SOCl₂ → RCl + SO₂ + HCl | Best: by-products are gases |

- Lucas test: conc. HCl + anhydrous ZnCl₂. ZnCl₂ (Lewis acid) helps –OH leave; the alkyl chloride is insoluble, so turbidity appears. 3° at once (SN1, stable carbocation); 2° within about 5 minutes; 1° none at room temperature.

O4. Oxidation of alcohols

| Alcohol | Reagent | Product | Why |
| 1° | PCC | Aldehyde | Mild; stops at the aldehyde |
| 1° | Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ or KMnO₄ | Carboxylic acid (via aldehyde) | Aldehyde is oxidised further |
| 2° | Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ | Ketone | One H on the carbinol carbon |
| 3° | Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ | No reaction | No H on the carbinol carbon |
| 1° / 2° / 3° | Cu, 573 K | Aldehyde / ketone / alkene | Dehydrogenation (1°, 2°); dehydration (3°) |

- Key role: the H on the carbon bearing –OH is what the oxidant removes. Cr(VI) (orange) is reduced to Cr(III) (green).

O5. Alcohols from alkenes

| Reaction | Reagents | Orientation | Roles |
| Acid-catalysed hydration | H₂O, H⁺ (dil. H₂SO₄) | Markovnikov | H⁺ electrophile first → carbocation; H₂O nucleophile |
| Hydroboration–oxidation | B₂H₆, then H₂O₂/OH⁻ | Anti-Markovnikov | B (electron-deficient) adds to the less substituted carbon, H to the other; H₂O₂ replaces B by OH |

- Example: propene → propan-2-ol (acid hydration) but propan-1-ol (hydroboration–oxidation).

O6. Esterification

Equation: RCOOH + R′OH ⇌ RCOOR′ + H₂O (conc. H₂SO₄).

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| O of the alcohol | Nucleophile; stays in the ester | Attacks the protonated carbonyl carbon |
| C of COOH | Electrophile | Protonation of C=O makes it more δ+ |
| –OH of the acid | Leaves as H₂O | The acid, not the alcohol, loses –OH |

- Reversible: remove water or use excess alcohol to push it forward.

O7. Williamson ether synthesis

Equation: RO⁻Na⁺ + R′–X → R–O–R′ + NaX (SN2).

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| O (alkoxide) | Nucleophile | Negative charge, lone pairs |
| C of R′X | Electrophile | δ+ next to halogen |
| X | Leaving group |  |

- Use a 1° alkyl halide. With a 3° halide the alkoxide acts as a base and gives an alkene (elimination).
- For (CH₃)₃C–O–CH₃: use (CH₃)₃CO⁻Na⁺ + CH₃Br, not (CH₃)₃CBr + CH₃O⁻Na⁺.
- Aryl ethers: phenoxide + alkyl halide (the aryl halide cannot be the electrophile).

O8. Cleavage of ethers by HI

Equation: R–O–R′ + HI → ROH + R′I.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| O | Base: protonated first | Lone pairs |
| I⁻ | Nucleophile | Good nucleophile; attacks the less hindered alkyl carbon (SN2) |
| Alkyl group | Electrophile | Gets I |

- Anisole + HI → phenol + CH₃I. The O–aryl bond (partial double bond, sp² carbon) is not broken.
- With a 3° alkyl group the cleavage goes by SN1 and the 3° group becomes the halide.

P1. Acidity of phenol

Equations: C₆H₅OH + NaOH → C₆H₅O⁻Na⁺ + H₂O; phenol + NaHCO₃ → no reaction.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| H (of O–H) | Acidic proton | sp² carbon pulls electrons; phenoxide is stabilised |
| O in phenoxide | Negative charge delocalised into the ring | Resonance |

- Acidity: carboxylic acid > phenol > water > alcohol.
- –NO₂ at o/p raises acidity (o- and p-nitrophenol > m-nitrophenol > phenol); –CH₃ lowers it.

P2. Bromination of phenol

| Conditions | Product | Why |
| Bromine water | 2,4,6-Tribromophenol (white precipitate) | Polar water polarises Br₂ and ionises phenol; ring strongly activated |
| Br₂ in CS₂ or CHCl₃, low temperature | o- and p-Bromophenol | Non-polar solvent: weaker electrophile, monosubstitution |

- Role of O: +R donor (lone pair into the ring), making ortho and para positions electron-rich. Lewis acid is not needed.

P3. Nitration of phenol

| Conditions | Product |
| Dilute HNO₃, 298 K | o- and p-Nitrophenol (separated by steam distillation: ortho is steam volatile due to intramolecular hydrogen bonding) |
| Conc. HNO₃ | 2,4,6-Trinitrophenol (picric acid) |

P4. Reimer–Tiemann reaction

Equation: phenol + CHCl₃ + aq. NaOH, then H⁺ → salicylaldehyde (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde).

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| CHCl₃ | Source of the electrophile (dichlorocarbene, :CCl₂) | OH⁻ removes H⁺, then Cl⁻ leaves |
| Phenoxide ring | Nucleophile, attacks at ortho | Strongly activated by O⁻ |
| –CHCl₂ group | Hydrolysed to –CHO | Aqueous alkali |

P5. Kolbe's reaction

Equation: sodium phenoxide + CO₂ (heat, pressure), then H⁺ → salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid).

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| Phenoxide ring | Nucleophile, attacks at ortho | Phenoxide is far more reactive than phenol |
| CO₂ | Weak electrophile | Carbon is δ+ between two O atoms |

P6. Other phenol reactions

- Phenol + Zn dust, heat → benzene (Zn removes O).
- Neutral FeCl₃ → violet colour (test for phenols).
- Phenol → no reaction with HCl; the C–O bond does not break.

Aldehydes, ketones and carboxylic acids

C1. Nucleophilic addition to C=O

General path: nucleophile attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon → carbon becomes sp³ (tetrahedral alkoxide) → O⁻ picks up H⁺.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| C of C=O | Electrophile | O (3.44) pulls electrons from C (2.55) |
| O of C=O | Takes the negative charge, then H⁺ | Electronegative; holds the π pair |
| Nucleophile | Attacks C | CN⁻, H⁻, R⁻ (Grignard), HSO₃⁻, ammonia derivatives |

| Reagent | Product | Note |
| HCN (base catalyst) | Cyanohydrin | CN⁻ is the nucleophile; base generates it |
| NaHSO₃ | Bisulphite addition product (crystalline) | Used to separate/purify aldehydes and some ketones |
| NH₂OH | Oxime | Addition, then loss of H₂O |
| NH₂NH₂ | Hydrazone | Addition, then loss of H₂O |
| 2,4-DNP | 2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazone (orange-red solid) | Test for C=O |
| R′OH, dry HCl | Hemiacetal → acetal (aldehydes); ketones with ethylene glycol → ketal |  |

- Reactivity: HCHO > other aldehydes > ketones (steric hindrance and +I of alkyl groups). Benzaldehyde is less reactive than propanal (resonance with the ring).

C2. Grignard addition

| Substrate | Product after H₃O⁺ |
| HCHO | 1° alcohol |
| RCHO (other aldehydes) | 2° alcohol |
| R₂CO (ketones) | 3° alcohol |
| CO₂ | Carboxylic acid (one carbon more) |
| RC≡N | Ketone |

- Roles: R⁻ of RMgX is the nucleophile on the carbonyl carbon; MgX⁺ binds the O; acid work-up supplies H⁺.

C3. Reductions of C=O

| Reagent | Change | Medium | Role |
| NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄ | Aldehyde → 1° alcohol; ketone → 2° alcohol |  | H⁻ nucleophile |
| H₂, Ni/Pt/Pd | Same as above |  | Catalyst |
| Zn–Hg, conc. HCl (Clemmensen) | C=O → CH₂ | Acidic | Zn is the reducing metal |
| NH₂NH₂, then KOH/ethylene glycol, heat (Wolff–Kishner) | C=O → CH₂ | Basic | Hydrazone loses N₂ |
| LiAlH₄ or B₂H₆ | RCOOH → RCH₂OH |  | NaBH₄ does not reduce acids |

- Choose Clemmensen for base-sensitive compounds and Wolff–Kishner for acid-sensitive ones.

C4. Oxidation tests: aldehydes vs ketones

| Test | Reagent | Positive result | Aldehydes | Ketones |
| Tollens' | [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ | Silver mirror | All (including aromatic) | No |
| Fehling's | Cu²⁺ (alkaline tartrate) | Red-brown Cu₂O | Aliphatic only (not benzaldehyde) | No |

- Role of H: the aldehyde's C–H on the carbonyl carbon makes it oxidisable to RCOO⁻. Ketones have no such H; strong oxidants break C–C bonds instead.
- Role of the metal: Ag⁺ → Ag (mirror); Cu²⁺ → Cu₂O.

C5. Aldol condensation

Equation: 2CH₃CHO (dil. NaOH) → CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CHO (aldol); heat → CH₃CH=CHCHO (but-2-enal) + H₂O.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| OH⁻ | Base: removes an α-H | α-H is acidic |
| α-Carbon | Becomes a carbanion (enolate), then a nucleophile | Resonance with C=O stabilises it |
| Carbonyl C of a second molecule | Electrophile | δ+ |

- Needs at least one α-H. Two different carbonyls with α-H give four products (cross aldol).
- Ketones with α-H also react (acetone → 4-hydroxy-4-methylpentan-2-one).

C6. Haloform (iodoform) reaction

Equation: CH₃COR + 3I₂ + 4NaOH → CHI₃↓ + RCOONa + 3NaI + 3H₂O.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| H atoms of CH₃ next to C=O | Replaced by I one by one | α-H is acidic in base |
| CI₃ group | Leaving group (CI₃⁻), becomes CHI₃ | Three I atoms stabilise the carbanion |
| OH⁻ | Base, then nucleophile on C=O |  |

- Positive for CH₃CO– compounds and for alcohols with CH₃CH(OH)– (oxidised to CH₃CO– first). Ethanol and acetaldehyde are the only 1° alcohol and aldehyde that give it. Methanol and HCHO do not.

C7. Cannizzaro reaction

Equation: 2HCHO + conc. NaOH → CH₃OH + HCOONa. 2C₆H₅CHO + conc. NaOH → C₆H₅CH₂OH + C₆H₅COONa.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| OH⁻ | Nucleophile on C=O of one molecule | No α-H, so it cannot act as a base for aldol |
| H on that carbonyl carbon | Transferred as hydride (H⁻) to the second molecule | Self oxidation–reduction (disproportionation) |
| Second aldehyde | Reduced to alcohol | Accepts H⁻ |

- Only aldehydes without α-H. Crossed Cannizzaro with HCHO: HCHO is oxidised (to formate) and the other aldehyde is reduced.

C9. Carboxylic acids

| Reaction | Equation / reagents | Role / note |
| Acidity | RCOOH ⇌ RCOO⁻ + H⁺ | Carboxylate is stabilised by two equivalent resonance structures; −I groups raise acidity, +I lower it |
| With NaHCO₃ | RCOOH + NaHCO₃ → RCOONa + H₂O + CO₂↑ | Test that separates acids from phenols |
| Acid chlorides | SOCl₂, PCl₅ or PCl₃ | –OH replaced by –Cl |
| Anhydride | 2RCOOH (P₂O₅ or H⁺, heat) → (RCO)₂O | Dehydration |

Amines and diazonium salts

A1. Hofmann bromamide degradation

Equation: RCONH₂ + Br₂ + 4NaOH → RNH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2NaBr + 2H₂O.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| N–H of the amide | Acidic; replaced by Br (N-bromoamide) | Lone pair delocalised into C=O makes N–H acidic |
| R group | Migrates from carbon to nitrogen | Forms an isocyanate (R–N=C=O) |
| Carbonyl carbon | Leaves as carbonate | One carbon fewer in the amine |
| Br | Leaving group from N |  |

- Gives 1° amines only, with one carbon fewer than the amide.

A2. Gabriel phthalimide synthesis

Steps: phthalimide + KOH → potassium phthalimide; + R–X → N-alkylphthalimide (SN2); alkaline hydrolysis → RNH₂.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| N–H of phthalimide | Acidic, removed by KOH | Flanked by two C=O groups |
| Phthalimide N⁻ | Nucleophile | Attacks the alkyl halide |
| R–X | Electrophile | Must be able to undergo SN2 |

- Only 1° aliphatic amines; no aromatic amines (aryl halides do not do SN2), and no over-alkylation.

A3. Carbylamine (isocyanide) test

Equation: RNH₂ + CHCl₃ + 3KOH (alc., heat) → RNC + 3KCl + 3H₂O. Foul-smelling isocyanide.

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| CHCl₃ + KOH | Generate dichlorocarbene (:CCl₂), the electrophile |  |
| N of 1° amine | Nucleophile; loses both H atoms | Needs two N–H |

- Given by 1° amines only (aliphatic and aromatic).

A4. Hinsberg test

| Amine | Reaction with C₆H₅SO₂Cl | Product behaviour in alkali |
| 1° | N attacks S; Cl⁻ leaves → C₆H₅SO₂NHR | Soluble (remaining N–H is acidic next to SO₂) |
| 2° | → C₆H₅SO₂NR₂ | Insoluble (no N–H) |
| 3° | No reaction | – |

- Role of N: nucleophile (lone pair) attacking sulphur; role of Cl: leaving group.

A5. Reaction with nitrous acid; diazotisation

Reagent: NaNO₂ + HCl → HNO₂, which gives the electrophile NO⁺.

| Amine | Conditions | Product |
| 1° aliphatic | Cold | Unstable diazonium salt → alcohol + N₂↑ (quantitative) |
| 1° aromatic (aniline) | 273–278 K | Benzenediazonium chloride C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ |

| Participant | Role here | Why |
| N of aniline | Nucleophile | Attacks NO⁺ |
| N₂⁺ group (later) | Excellent leaving group (N₂ gas) | Very stable N≡N molecule |

- Kept cold: the aromatic diazonium salt is stable only at low temperature (resonance with the ring).

A6. Replacement of the diazonium group

A7. Azo coupling

| Partner | Medium | Product | Colour |
| Phenol | Alkaline | p-Hydroxyazobenzene | Orange |
| Aniline | Mildly acidic | p-Aminoazobenzene | Yellow |

- Roles: diazonium ion is a weak electrophile; the strongly activated ring (phenoxide, aniline) is the nucleophile, attacking at para. N₂ is retained (–N=N– link).

A9. Making amines by reduction

| Starting compound | Reagent | Product |
