Give reasons

1. Phenol is more acidic than ethanol.

Phenoxide's negative charge is delocalised over the ring by resonance, which stabilises it. Ethoxide's charge stays on O and is intensified by the +I effect of the ethyl group. The more stable the conjugate base, the stronger the acid (P1).

2. Aniline is a weaker base than methylamine.

In aniline the N lone pair is delocalised into the ring, so it is less available for a proton. In methylamine, the +I effect of CH₃ increases electron density on N.

3. Tertiary butyl bromide reacts by SN1 faster than ethyl bromide.

It forms a 3° carbocation, stabilised by +I and hyperconjugation (nine α-H). The bulky groups also block backside attack, so SN2 is not available to it (X1, X2).

4. Aldehydes are more reactive than ketones towards nucleophilic addition.

An aldehyde has one alkyl group, a ketone two. Two alkyl groups reduce the carbonyl carbon's positive character (+I) and crowd the approaching nucleophile (C1).

5. Chlorobenzene is far less reactive than chloromethane towards nucleophilic substitution.

In chlorobenzene the C–Cl bond has partial double-bond character (resonance with the ring); the carbon is sp² (shorter, stronger bond); the electron-rich ring repels the nucleophile; and the phenyl cation is unstable, so SN1 is impossible too (X7).

6. Haloalkanes give alcohols with aqueous KOH but alkenes with alcoholic KOH.

In water, OH⁻ is heavily solvated and acts as a nucleophile (substitution). In alcohol, the alkoxide/hydroxide ion is a stronger base and removes a β-hydrogen (elimination) (X3).

7. Carboxylic acids do not give the typical reactions of the carbonyl group.

The C=O of –COOH is in resonance with the lone pair of the –OH oxygen, which lowers the electrophilic character of the carbonyl carbon (C9).

8. Aniline does not undergo Friedel–Crafts reactions.

The lone pair on N reacts with the Lewis acid AlCl₃ to form a salt, putting a positive charge on N, which strongly deactivates the ring (A8).

Identify the unknown

1. A compound C₄H₈O forms an orange precipitate with 2,4-DNP, gives no silver mirror with Tollens', and gives a yellow precipitate with I₂/NaOH. Identify it.

1. DU = (2×4 + 2 − 8)/2 = 1: one C=O fits.
2. 2,4-DNP positive → aldehyde or ketone. Carbonyl isomers of C₄H₈O: butanal, 2-methylpropanal, butan-2-one.
3. Tollens' negative → not an aldehyde → butan-2-one.
4. Iodoform positive → has CH₃CO–. Butan-2-one (CH₃COCH₂CH₃) fits.
5. Answer: butan-2-one.

2. Compound A (C₂H₆O) releases H₂ with sodium, gives the iodoform test, and on oxidation gives an acid that turns NaHCO₃ effervescent. Identify A.

1. C₂H₆O isomers: ethanol and methoxymethane.
2. H₂ with Na → O–H present → ethanol (the ether has no O–H).
3. Iodoform positive (CH₃CH(OH)–) and oxidation to ethanoic acid confirm it.
4. Answer: ethanol.

3. An aromatic compound C₇H₆O₂ gives effervescence with NaHCO₃. A second isomer gives a silver mirror with Tollens' and a violet colour with FeCl₃. Identify both.

1. DU = (2×7 + 2 − 6)/2 = 5: benzene ring (4) + one C=O.
2. NaHCO₃ effervescence → –COOH → benzoic acid, C₆H₅COOH.
3. Silver mirror → –CHO; violet with FeCl₃ → phenolic –OH. Ring + CHO + OH = C₇H₆O₂ → a hydroxybenzaldehyde, e.g. salicylaldehyde (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde, from Reimer–Tiemann, P4).

4. An amine C₃H₉N gives a foul smell with CHCl₃ and alcoholic KOH. With nitrous acid it gives an alcohol that gives the iodoform test. Identify the amine.

1. Carbylamine positive → 1° amine: propan-1-amine or propan-2-amine.
2. With HNO₂, a 1° aliphatic amine gives the corresponding alcohol (A5): propan-1-ol or propan-2-ol.
3. Only propan-2-ol has CH₃CH(OH)– and gives iodoform.
4. Answer: propan-2-amine, (CH₃)₂CHNH₂.

Write the mechanism

1. Acid-catalysed dehydration of ethanol to ethene (443 K).

1. Protonation: a lone pair on O attacks H⁺ → CH₃CH₂–OH₂⁺ (fast).
2. Water leaves: the C–O bond breaks, the electrons go with O → CH₃CH₂⁺ + H₂O (slow, rate-determining).
3. Loss of H⁺: a β-C–H bond's electrons move to form C=C; H⁺ is released → CH₂=CH₂.

2. Nucleophilic addition of HCN to ethanal.

1. Base generates the nucleophile: HCN + OH⁻ → CN⁻ + H₂O.
2. CN⁻ attacks the carbonyl carbon; the C=O π electrons move onto O → tetrahedral alkoxide (slow).
3. O⁻ takes H⁺ from HCN (or water) → CH₃CH(OH)CN, ethanal cyanohydrin.

3. Nitration of benzene.

1. Electrophile: HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻.
2. The ring's π electrons attack NO₂⁺ → arenium ion (σ-complex; one sp³ carbon carries H and NO₂; positive charge delocalised).
3. HSO₄⁻ removes H⁺ from that carbon; the electrons restore the aromatic ring → nitrobenzene.

4. SN2 of bromomethane with hydroxide.

1. OH⁻ approaches the carbon from the side opposite Br.
2. Transition state: HO···C···Br, with the three H atoms flattened in one plane.
3. Br⁻ leaves as the C–O bond forms; the H atoms flip (inversion) → CH₃OH.
