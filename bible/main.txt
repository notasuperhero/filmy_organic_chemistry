Carbon and hydrogen

Carbon (C)

What it does

- Tetravalent; shows catenation (chains, branches, rings).
- Hybridisation sets shape and bond length:

| Hybridisation | Bond angle | Shape | Found in | s-character | C–C bond length |
| sp³ | 109°28′ | Tetrahedral | Alkanes, alkyl groups | 25% | 154 pm |
| sp² | 120° | Trigonal planar | Alkenes, C=O carbon, benzene, carbocations | 33% | 134 pm (C=C), 139 pm (benzene) |
| sp | 180° | Linear | Alkynes, nitriles | 50% | 120 pm (C≡C) |

- More s-character makes carbon more electronegative: sp > sp² > sp³. This makes the C–H of ethyne the most acidic of the three hydrocarbons.
- Bond fission: homolytic (light, heat, peroxide) gives free radicals; heterolytic gives a carbocation and a carbanion.

Reactive intermediates

| Intermediate | Electrons on C | Hybridisation / shape | Behaves as | Stability order |
| Carbocation | 6 | sp², planar | Electrophile | 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺; benzylic and allylic extra stable (resonance) |
| Carbanion | 8 | sp³, pyramidal | Nucleophile, base | CH₃⁻ > 1° > 2° > 3° |
| Free radical | 7 | sp², planar | Reacts by chain mechanism | 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃• |

- Carbocations rearrange by 1,2-hydride or 1,2-methyl shift to a more stable carbocation.
- Carbocation and radical stability come from +I of alkyl groups and hyperconjugation; benzyl and allyl from resonance.

What it doesn't do

- Never has five bonds in a stable compound; five partial bonds exist only in the SN2 transition state.
- A flat carbocation keeps no memory of its old configuration: SN1 at a chiral carbon gives racemisation.

Hydrogen (H)

What it does

- Acidity of an H depends on what it is attached to (approximate pKa):

| Compound type | Approx. pKa | Reacts with NaHCO₃? | Reacts with NaOH? | Reacts with Na metal? |
| Carboxylic acid | 4–5 | Yes (CO₂ effervescence) | Yes | Yes |
| Phenol | 10 | No | Yes | Yes |
| Water | 15.7 | – | – | Yes |
| Alcohol | 16–18 | No | No | Yes (H₂ released) |
| Terminal alkyne (≡C–H) | 25 | No | No | Yes; also NaNH₂ |
| Ammonia / amine N–H | ~38 | No | No | Slowly |
| Alkene C–H | ~44 | No | No | No |
| Alkane C–H | ~50 | No | No | No |

- α-Hydrogen (on the carbon next to C=O) is acidic, because the enolate is resonance-stabilised. Carbonyls with α-H undergo aldol condensation and halogenation; those without α-H (HCHO, C₆H₅CHO, (CH₃)₃C–CHO) undergo Cannizzaro.
- Hyperconjugation: C–H bonds on the carbon next to a carbocation or C=C donate σ-electron density. More α-H, more stability: (CH₃)₃C⁺ has 9 α-H.
- Hydride (H⁻) donors: LiAlH₄ and NaBH₄ deliver H⁻ to the electrophilic carbon of C=O.
- Markovnikov: in HX addition, H goes to the double-bond carbon that already has more H atoms.

What it doesn't do

- Alkanes and alkenes give no H₂ with sodium; only acidic H (O–H, ≡C–H) does.
- Internal alkynes (no ≡C–H) do not form acetylides.

Oxygen and nitrogen

Oxygen (O)

What it does

- sp³ in alcohols and ethers (bent; C–O–H 108.9° in methanol, C–O–C 111.7° in dimethyl ether); sp² in C=O.
- Two lone pairs: Lewis base, nucleophile, hydrogen bonding. Alcohols boil far higher than ethers or alkanes of similar mass; lower alcohols dissolve in water.
- Electronic effects: −I always. –OH and –OR on a benzene ring are +R: activating, ortho/para directing. A C=O group (–CHO, –COR, –COOH) is −R: deactivating, meta directing.
- C=O is polar (C δ+, O δ−), so nucleophiles add to the carbon. Reactivity: HCHO > CH₃CHO > CH₃COCH₃ (aldehydes beat ketones for steric and +I reasons).
- Phenol is more acidic than alcohol: phenoxide is resonance-stabilised. −R/−I groups (–NO₂) at o/p raise phenol's acidity; +I groups (–CH₃) lower it.

Reactions

- Alcohol + Na → sodium alkoxide + ½H₂.
- Ethanol + conc. H₂SO₄: 443 K → ethene; 413 K → diethyl ether.
- Ease of dehydration: 3° > 2° > 1° (via carbocation).
- Lucas test (conc. HCl + anhydrous ZnCl₂): 3° turbid at once; 2° within about 5 minutes; 1° no turbidity at room temperature.
- Oxidation: 1° alcohol → aldehyde (PCC) → carboxylic acid (KMnO₄ or acidified K₂Cr₂O₇); 2° → ketone; 3° resists.
- Cu at 573 K: 1° → aldehyde, 2° → ketone (dehydrogenation); 3° → alkene (dehydration).
- Esterification: RCOOH + R′OH ⇌ RCOOR′ + H₂O (conc. H₂SO₄). The acid loses –OH; the alcohol keeps its oxygen.
- Williamson synthesis: RO⁻Na⁺ + R′X → ROR′ (SN2). Use a 1° alkyl halide; with a 3° halide, elimination gives an alkene.
- Ether cleavage with HI: R–O–R′ → ROH + R′I. Anisole + HI → phenol + CH₃I (never iodobenzene).
- Phenol: Kolbe's reaction (sodium phenoxide + CO₂, then H⁺) → salicylic acid; Reimer–Tiemann (CHCl₃ + aq. NaOH, then H⁺) → salicylaldehyde; nitration with dilute HNO₃ at 298 K → o- and p-nitrophenol; conc. HNO₃ → picric acid (2,4,6-trinitrophenol).
- Ozonolysis: alkene + O₃, then Zn/H₂O → two carbonyl compounds (locates the C=C).

What it doesn't do

- –OH is a poor leaving group. It must be protonated (–OH₂⁺) or converted (SOCl₂, PCl₅, HCl/ZnCl₂) before substitution.
- Phenol does not react with NaHCO₃; carboxylic acids do.
- The C–O bond of phenol does not break easily (partial double-bond character), so phenol does not give chlorobenzene with HCl.
- Carboxylic acids do not show typical aldehyde/ketone addition reactions (C=O is resonance-stabilised).

Nitrogen (N)

What it does

- sp³ in amines (pyramidal, one lone pair); lone pair delocalised in amides (nearly neutral); sp in nitriles.
- Basicity: aliphatic amines > NH₃ > aromatic amines (the lone pair of aniline is delocalised into the ring).
- In water: (CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂ > (CH₃)₃N > NH₃, and (C₂H₅)₂NH > (C₂H₅)₃N > C₂H₅NH₂ > NH₃ (+I, solvation and steric effects together).
- –NH₂ on benzene: strongly activating, o/p directing (+R). –NO₂: −I and −R, strongly deactivating, meta directing; at o/p it activates a haloarene for nucleophilic substitution.

Reactions

- Hofmann bromamide degradation: RCONH₂ + Br₂ + 4NaOH → RNH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2NaBr + 2H₂O (amine has one carbon fewer).
- Gabriel phthalimide synthesis: phthalimide + KOH, then RX, then hydrolysis → 1° aliphatic amine only.
- Reduction: RC≡N + H₂/Ni or LiAlH₄ → RCH₂NH₂; ArNO₂ + Fe/HCl or Sn/HCl → ArNH₂; RCONH₂ + LiAlH₄ → RCH₂NH₂.
- Ammonolysis of RX gives a mixture of 1°, 2°, 3° amines and the quaternary salt.
- Carbylamine test (1° amines only): RNH₂ + CHCl₃ + 3KOH (alc.) → RNC (foul smell) + 3KCl + 3H₂O.
- Hinsberg test (C₆H₅SO₂Cl): 1° → sulphonamide soluble in alkali; 2° → sulphonamide insoluble in alkali; 3° → no reaction.
- Nitrous acid: 1° aliphatic amine → alcohol + N₂ (quantitative); aniline at 273–278 K → benzenediazonium chloride (diazotisation).
- Diazonium replacements: CuCl/HCl, CuBr/HBr, CuCN/KCN (Sandmeyer); Cu/HX (Gattermann); KI → ArI; HBF₄ then heat → ArF; H₃PO₂ or ethanol → ArH; warm water → phenol.
- Coupling: with phenol (alkaline) → p-hydroxyazobenzene (orange); with aniline (mildly acidic) → p-aminoazobenzene (yellow).
- Aniline + bromine water → 2,4,6-tribromoaniline (white precipitate). For a mono product, acetylate first (–NHCOCH₃ is a milder activator).
- Wolff–Kishner: C=O + NH₂NH₂, then KOH in ethylene glycol, heat → CH₂.
- Nitration mixture (conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄) generates the electrophile NO₂⁺.

What it doesn't do

- Aniline does not undergo Friedel–Crafts: its lone pair binds AlCl₃ and deactivates the ring.
- Direct nitration of aniline is not clean: in acid it becomes anilinium (meta directing), giving about 51% para, 47% meta and 2% ortho product.
- Gabriel synthesis cannot make aromatic amines (aryl halides do not undergo SN with phthalimide anion).
- 2° and 3° amines do not give the carbylamine test.

Halogens: F, Cl, Br, I

Shared behaviour

- −I effect: F > Cl > Br > I. So acidity: FCH₂COOH > ClCH₂COOH > BrCH₂COOH > ICH₂COOH > CH₃COOH.
- On a benzene ring, halogens are deactivating (−I wins) but ortho/para directing (+R).
- C–X bond strength: C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I. Leaving ability and reactivity of R–X in SN1/SN2: R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F.
- Dipole moments: CH₃Cl (1.860 D) > CH₃F (1.847 D) > CH₃Br (1.830 D) > CH₃I (1.636 D).
- Haloarenes resist nucleophilic substitution: the C–X bond has partial double-bond character (resonance) and the carbon is sp². –NO₂ at ortho/para makes substitution easy.
- Haloalkanes: aqueous KOH → substitution (alcohol); alcoholic KOH → elimination (alkene, Saytzeff product major).
- Ambident nucleophiles: KCN → nitrile (R–CN), AgCN → isocyanide (R–NC); KNO₂ → alkyl nitrite (R–O–N=O), AgNO₂ → nitroalkane (R–NO₂).
- Lassaigne's test: halide ion as NaX → boil with HNO₃, then AgNO₃. AgCl white (soluble in NH₃), AgBr pale yellow (sparingly soluble), AgI yellow (insoluble).
- Alkyl halides give no direct precipitate with AgNO₃ (covalent C–X).

Fluorine (F)

- Highest electronegativity (3.98); strongest −I; strongest C–X bond; poorest leaving group.
- Direct fluorination is violent. Alkyl fluorides are made by the Swarts reaction: R–Cl/R–Br + AgF, Hg₂F₂, CoF₂ or SbF₃ → R–F.
- Aryl fluorides: diazonium salt + HBF₄, then heat → ArF.

Chlorine (Cl)

- Free-radical chlorination of alkanes (Cl₂, hν): chain reaction, gives mixtures; less selective than bromination.
- Benzene + Cl₂ with anhydrous FeCl₃ or AlCl₃ → chlorobenzene (electrophilic substitution).
- Toluene + Cl₂: with FeCl₃ → o- and p-chlorotoluene (ring); in sunlight or heat → benzyl chloride (side chain).
- Alcohol → alkyl chloride: SOCl₂ (best; by-products SO₂ and HCl escape as gases), PCl₅, PCl₃, or HCl with anhydrous ZnCl₂.
- Chlorobenzene → phenol needs NaOH at 623 K and 300 atm (Dow process).
- Chloroform slowly oxidises in air and light to poisonous phosgene: 2CHCl₃ + O₂ → 2COCl₂ + 2HCl. Stored in dark, completely filled bottles.
- Chloroform + 3KOH (alc.) with a 1° amine: carbylamine test. With phenol + NaOH: Reimer–Tiemann.

Bromine (Br)

- Br₂ in CCl₄ adds across C=C and C≡C; loss of the red-brown colour is the test for unsaturation.
- HBr + unsymmetrical alkene: Markovnikov product. With peroxide: anti-Markovnikov (Kharasch or peroxide effect), by a free-radical mechanism. The peroxide effect works for HBr only, not HCl or HI.
- Radical bromination is more selective than chlorination: it strongly prefers 3° H.
- Phenol + bromine water → 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white precipitate). Phenol + Br₂ in CS₂ at low temperature → o- and p-bromophenol.
- Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky: RCH₂COOH + X₂ (Cl₂ or Br₂), red P, then H₂O → RCH(X)COOH. Needs an α-H.
- Br₂ + NaOH converts an amide to an amine (Hofmann bromamide).

Iodine (I)

- Weakest C–X bond: alkyl iodides are the most reactive in substitution.
- Finkelstein reaction: R–Cl/R–Br + NaI in dry acetone → R–I. NaCl/NaBr precipitates and drives the reaction forward.
- Direct iodination of benzene is reversible; it needs an oxidising agent (HNO₃ or HIO₄) to remove HI.
- Iodoform test: compounds with CH₃CO– or CH₃CH(OH)– + I₂/NaOH → CHI₃ (yellow precipitate). Ethanol is the only 1° alcohol and acetaldehyde the only aldehyde that give it.
- HI cleaves ethers; it is the strongest of the HX acids for this.
- Diazonium salt + KI → aryl iodide (no copper needed).

Sulphur and phosphorus

Sulphur (S)

- Conc. H₂SO₄: dehydrating agent (alcohol → alkene or ether), catalyst for esterification, and the acid that generates NO₂⁺ in the nitrating mixture.
- Sulphonation: benzene + fuming H₂SO₄ (electrophile SO₃) → benzenesulphonic acid. Sulphonation is reversible.
- Thionyl chloride: ROH + SOCl₂ → RCl + SO₂↑ + HCl↑ (pure product, gases escape). RCOOH + SOCl₂ → RCOCl.
- Benzenesulphonyl chloride (C₆H₅SO₂Cl) is Hinsberg's reagent for telling 1°, 2° and 3° amines apart.
- Lassaigne's test: S becomes Na₂S. Sodium nitroprusside → violet colour; lead acetate → black PbS. N and S together give NaSCN → blood-red colour with Fe³⁺.

Phosphorus (P)

- PCl₅: ROH + PCl₅ → RCl + POCl₃ + HCl; RCOOH + PCl₅ → RCOCl + POCl₃ + HCl.
- PCl₃: 3ROH + PCl₃ → 3RCl + H₃PO₃; 3RCOOH + PCl₃ → 3RCOCl + H₃PO₃.
- Red P + Br₂ or I₂ forms PBr₃ or PI₃ in situ: converts alcohols to RBr or RI; with Br₂ it is the catalyst in the Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky reaction.
- P₂O₅ dehydrates amides to nitriles: RCONH₂ → RCN + H₂O.
- Lassaigne's test: P is oxidised to phosphate; ammonium molybdate gives a yellow precipitate.
- Replacing the –OH of phenol by halogen with PCl₅ is poor (low yield); phenols are not converted to haloarenes this way in practice.

Li, Na, K, Mg, B, Al

Lithium (Li)

- Appears as LiAlH₄, a strong hydride (H⁻) donor.
- LiAlH₄ reduces: aldehydes → 1° alcohols; ketones → 2° alcohols; carboxylic acids and esters → 1° alcohols; nitriles and amides → amines.
- It does not reduce an isolated C=C.

Sodium (Na)

- Na + ROH → RONa + ½H₂; Na (or NaNH₂) + terminal alkyne → sodium acetylide.
- Wurtz reaction: 2RX + 2Na (dry ether) → R–R + 2NaX. Gives symmetric alkanes; two different halides give a mixture; methane cannot be made.
- Wurtz–Fittig: ArX + RX + 2Na (dry ether) → Ar–R. Fittig: 2ArX + 2Na → Ar–Ar.
- Decarboxylation: RCOONa + NaOH (sodalime, CaO), heat → RH + Na₂CO₃ (one carbon fewer).
- Kolbe electrolysis: 2RCOONa(aq) → R–R + 2CO₂ at the anode (+ H₂ and NaOH at the cathode). Methane cannot be made.
- Na in liquid NH₃ reduces alkynes to trans-alkenes (Lindlar's catalyst gives cis).
- NaBH₄: reduces aldehydes and ketones to alcohols; does not reduce carboxylic acids or esters.
- NaHCO₃: effervescence (CO₂) with carboxylic acids, not with phenols.
- NaOH: dilute → aldol condensation (α-H present); concentrated → Cannizzaro (no α-H): 2HCHO + NaOH → CH₃OH + HCOONa. With I₂ → iodoform test.
- Sodium fusion (Lassaigne's test): converts N, S and halogens to NaCN, Na₂S and NaX. N detected as Prussian blue with FeSO₄, then Fe³⁺.

Potassium (K)

- KOH: aqueous → substitution of R–X (alcohol); alcoholic → dehydrohalogenation (alkene). Saytzeff rule: the more substituted alkene is the major product.
- Alcoholic KOH twice on a vicinal dihalide → alkyne (second step often needs NaNH₂).
- KCN → alkyl cyanide (adds one carbon); KNO₂ → alkyl nitrite.
- Potassium phthalimide: Gabriel synthesis of 1° aliphatic amines.
- KMnO₄ and K₂Cr₂O₇: see Mn and Cr.

Magnesium (Mg)

- Grignard reagent: R–X + Mg in dry ether → RMgX. The carbon bonded to Mg acts as a carbanion: a strong nucleophile and a strong base.
- With carbonyls, then hydrolysis: HCHO → 1° alcohol; any other aldehyde → 2° alcohol; ketone → 3° alcohol.
- With CO₂ (dry ice), then H₃O⁺ → carboxylic acid (one carbon more). With a nitrile, then hydrolysis → ketone.
- Any compound with acidic H (water, alcohol, amine N–H, terminal alkyne) destroys it: RMgX + H₂O → RH + Mg(OH)X. That is why the ether must be dry.

Boron (B)

- Electron-deficient (only 6 electrons in BH₃ and BF₃): a Lewis acid.
- Hydroboration–oxidation: alkene + B₂H₆, then H₂O₂/OH⁻ → alcohol with anti-Markovnikov orientation (OH on the less substituted carbon).
- B₂H₆ also reduces carboxylic acids to 1° alcohols.
- NaBH₄: see Na.

Aluminium (Al)

- Anhydrous AlCl₃ is a Lewis acid that makes the electrophile.
- Friedel–Crafts alkylation: C₆H₆ + RCl (AlCl₃) → C₆H₅R + HCl. Acylation: C₆H₆ + RCOCl (AlCl₃) → C₆H₅COR + HCl.
- Also catalyses halogenation of benzene (with Cl₂).
- Friedel–Crafts fails on strongly deactivated rings (nitrobenzene, benzoic acid) and on aniline.
- LiAlH₄: see Li.

Zn, Sn, Fe, Cu, Hg, Ag

Zinc (Zn)

- Clemmensen reduction: C=O + Zn–Hg / conc. HCl → CH₂.
- Phenol + Zn dust, heat → benzene.
- Anhydrous ZnCl₂ + conc. HCl = Lucas reagent.
- Ozonolysis work-up: Zn/H₂O decomposes the ozonide into aldehydes/ketones without oxidising them further.

Tin (Sn)

- Sn/HCl: ArNO₂ → ArNH₂.
- Stephen reduction: RCN + SnCl₂/HCl → imine, then H₃O⁺ → RCHO.

Iron (Fe)

- Fe/HCl: ArNO₂ → ArNH₂ (preferred: FeCl₂ formed is hydrolysed and releases HCl, so little acid is needed).
- Anhydrous FeCl₃ or FeBr₃: Lewis acid for halogenating benzene.
- Neutral FeCl₃ test: phenols give a violet colour.
- Ethyne passed through a red-hot iron tube at 873 K trimerises to benzene.

Copper (Cu)

- Cu at 573 K: 1° alcohol → aldehyde; 2° → ketone; 3° → alkene.
- Sandmeyer: ArN₂⁺Cl⁻ + CuCl/HCl → ArCl; CuBr/HBr → ArBr; CuCN/KCN → ArCN. Gattermann: Cu powder/HX → ArX.
- Fehling's solution (Cu²⁺ tartrate complex): aliphatic aldehydes give a red-brown Cu₂O precipitate. Aromatic aldehydes and ketones do not.
- Ammoniacal Cu₂Cl₂ + terminal alkyne → red copper acetylide (internal alkynes do not react).

Mercury (Hg)

- Alkyne hydration: alkyne + dil. H₂SO₄ with HgSO₄ at 333 K → carbonyl. Ethyne gives acetaldehyde; every other alkyne gives a ketone (Markovnikov).
- Zn–Hg amalgam: Clemmensen reduction.
- Hg₂F₂: reagent in the Swarts reaction.

Silver (Ag)

- Tollens' reagent ([Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺): all aldehydes, including aromatic ones, give a silver mirror. Ketones do not.
- AgCN → isocyanides; AgNO₂ → nitroalkanes; AgF → alkyl fluorides (Swarts).
- AgNO₃: detects halide ions in Lassaigne's test. Ammoniacal AgNO₃ + terminal alkyne → white silver acetylide.

Ni, Pd, Pt, Mn, Cr

Nickel, palladium and platinum (Ni, Pd, Pt)

- Catalytic hydrogenation: alkenes and alkynes + H₂ → alkanes. Pt or Pd work at room temperature; Ni needs 523–573 K.
- H₂/Ni also reduces nitriles to amines and aldehydes/ketones to alcohols.
- Lindlar's catalyst (palladised charcoal deactivated with sulphur compounds or quinoline): alkyne → cis-alkene, stops there.
- Rosenmund reduction: RCOCl + H₂, Pd on BaSO₄ → RCHO (the poisoned catalyst stops reduction at the aldehyde).

Manganese (Mn) as KMnO₄ (+7)

- Baeyer's reagent (cold, dilute, alkaline KMnO₄): alkene → vicinal diol. The pink colour disappears: test for unsaturation.
- Hot acidic or alkaline KMnO₄: cleaves C=C into carboxylic acids, ketones or CO₂, depending on substitution.
- Side-chain oxidation: any alkylbenzene with a benzylic H → benzoic acid, whatever the chain length. Tert-butylbenzene (no benzylic H) is not oxidised.
- Oxidises 1° alcohols and aldehydes to carboxylic acids.

Chromium (Cr)

- Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ (orange Cr₂O₇²⁻ → green Cr³⁺): 1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid; 2° → ketone; 3° no reaction.
- PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate): 1° alcohol → aldehyde, stops there.
- Étard reaction: toluene + CrO₂Cl₂ (in CS₂), then H₃O⁺ → benzaldehyde.
- Toluene + CrO₃ in acetic anhydride → benzylidene diacetate, then hydrolysis → benzaldehyde.
